# Is hypergamy real? A literature review

> [!summary] Short answer
> It depends on which "hypergamy" you mean.
> - **Stated preferences:** yes, on average women in almost every country say a partner's earning prospects matter more to them than men say. The difference is real but small on any single trait, and both sexes rank kindness and intelligence higher than money or looks.
> - **Marrying up in education:** it used to be the norm and has **reversed** in most of the world. Women are now more educated than men, and wives more often have more education than their husbands. Models show this follows supply, not a change in what people want.
> - **Marrying up in income and age:** still common. Husbands usually earn more and are about two to three years older, and the income gap persists even in couples where she has more education. How much of this is preference, the gender pay gap, or selection is debated.
> - **The online "80/20" claim** (most women chase a small top group of men): **not supported** as stated. Attention on dating apps is unequal and men get fewer replies, but both sexes message "up" by similar amounts, and actual couples are mostly matched like with like.
>
> **Beyond status (section 11):**
> - **Short vs long term:** the sex difference in caring about status is mostly a *long-term* preference. For short-term partners both sexes put looks first, and men lower their standards much more than women. Claims that women's tastes swing with the ovulatory cycle have not held up in large preregistered studies.
> - **Height and build:** women want taller men more than men want shorter women, and couples follow the "man taller" norm, but only a little more often than chance would give (about 1 couple in 13 breaks it). Perceived upper-body strength drives ratings of men's bodies in photo studies. There is no study on arm span.
> - **Penis size:** a small effect in lab ratings, about as large as height, with diminishing returns; body shape matters more. Most women say they are satisfied with their partner's size; men worry about it far more.
> - **Intelligence:** one of the most wanted traits for both sexes, with signs of a ceiling in stated ratings; partners are strongly matched on education and intelligence (like with like again).
> - **App data and race:** looks drive messages for both sexes; men's messages target younger women. Racial hierarchies in who gets contacted and answered are well documented, which is bias, not anyone's "value". No peer-reviewed measure of swipe inequality ("Gini of likes") exists; those numbers are blog-grade.
> - **Dark Triad ("bad boys"):** the evidence splits three ways. (1) **Partner counts: real.** People higher in narcissism and psychopathy (subclinical, measured by questionnaire) report more casual sex and more sexual partners, in men and in women, in surveys across 53 nations. (2) **Short-term pull of narcissistic confidence: real.** In real first meetings, street approaches and speed dating, narcissistic people of both sexes are chosen more, mostly because they look better, dress better and act bolder; women who want to marry, and women with many past partners, also report finding narcissistic traits attractive. (3) **Long-term preference and cruelty as such: not supported.** No study finds high scorers preferred as long-term partners, faces and profiles high in Machiavellianism and psychopathy are mostly disliked except by people who score high themselves, the largest randomised profile study found low levels preferred, and the traits predict cheating and lower satisfaction. Verdict on "women love bad boys": **true for flings and first impressions when "bad boy" means bold and self-promoting; false for long-term choice and for cruelty itself.** The same pattern holds for men choosing narcissistic women.
> - **Paternity and male ancestry:** "10 to 30% of children are not their father's" is wrong as a population figure. Genetic studies put it at about 1 to 2% (0.4% to 6% historically, higher among poor urban families); about 30% applies only to men who already doubted and paid for a test. No peer-reviewed rate exists for France, where private paternity tests are illegal; the nearest data (Flanders, the Low Countries) give about 1%. The male-ancestry signal is real: over the long run the female effective population size was about twice the male one, and about 8,000 to 4,000 years ago up to 17 times larger (karmin2015). That 17:1 is a genuine result, but it is a ratio of *effective* population sizes, not a count of 17 women having children for every man, and its cause (polygyny and status, clan warfare, conquest) is debated. Haplogroup N1c is carried by about 64% of Finnish men and about 40% of Latvian and Lithuanian men: a few founding male lines out-reproduced the others over thousands of years, which is different from a few living men fathering most children today.

## 1. Definitions

The word "hypergamy" is used for four different things. Most arguments online mix them up.

| # | Meaning | What would count as evidence | Typical data |
|---|---------|------------------------------|--------------|
| D1 | **Partnering up in status**: the woman's partner ranks above her on education, income, occupation, class or age | Share of couples where he is higher, compared with what random pairing would give | Census, registers, surveys of couples |
| D2 | **Stated preferences**: women say they weigh status or earnings more than men do | Questionnaire ratings and rankings | Cross-cultural surveys (Buss and successors) |
| D3 | **Revealed preferences and actual matching**: what people do when choosing | Who people contact, reply to, swipe on, date, marry | Dating-site logs, speed dating, field experiments, marriage records |
| D4 | **The strong online claim**: most women pursue a small top fraction of men ("80/20"), and status is all that matters | Concentration of attention and of actual partnering across men | App data (mostly unpublished), messaging networks |

D1 is a pattern in couples; D2 is a stated attitude; D3 is behaviour; D4 is a specific quantitative claim. Evidence for one does not prove another. A pattern of women marrying up (D1) can occur without anyone preferring it, simply because of who is available (see section 6), and a stated preference (D2) may predict little of who people actually like (section 4).

## 3. Stated preferences

**What the big surveys show.** Buss (1989) asked over 10,000 people in 37 samples to rate partner traits. Women rated "good financial prospects" and ambition higher than men did in most samples; men rated youth and looks higher. The 2020 replication in 45 countries (N = 14,399) found the same pattern (walter2020). In numbers: on a 1 to 7 scale, women rated financial prospects 0.30 points higher than men, and men rated physical attractiveness 0.27 points higher. Both sexes rated kindness, intelligence and health higher than either money or looks, and women asked slightly more of each of these.

**How big is "big"?** Single-trait differences are small. When all traits are combined into one multivariate distance, the difference looks large: D = 2.41 with only about 23% overlap across 18 items in the 1989 data (conroybeam2015), and D = 0.73 across 5 items in 2020 (walter2020). The number depends on how many traits you include and how they correlate. Honest reporting gives both: individual traits overlap heavily, the overall profile differs more.

**Necessities and luxuries.** When people must spend a small "budget" on partner traits, women spend first on status and resources and men on looks; with larger budgets the sexes look more alike (li2002). This is a stated trade-off task, not a choice of real partners.

**Gender equality.** Social role theory predicts the provider preference should shrink where women have their own resources (eagly1999, wood2002). Some data agree: women's preference for resource traits was strongest where women had little reproductive freedom and educational equality (kasser1999), and sex differences were smaller in more equal countries (zentner2012, conroybeam2015). But larger or more carefully controlled replications found little moderation except for age gaps (zhang2019, walter2020), and neighbouring countries are not independent data points (gangestad2006). The fair summary: the difference is present in every country studied; whether it shrinks with equality is unresolved.

## 4. Stated vs revealed preferences

What people *say* they want and whom they *choose* are different measurements.

- In speed dating, the stated sex difference in caring about earning prospects did not predict who people actually liked after meeting (eastwick2008).
- A meta-analysis of 97 studies found that a person's attractiveness predicted romantic interest at about r = .40 and their earning prospects at about r = .10, **for both sexes**, with a sex difference of about .03 (eastwick2014). Critics argue the meta-analysis covers early attraction and existing couples, not long-term choice from a wide pool, so the debate continues.
- At first contact, sex differences reappear. Women on an online site weighted a man's income more than men weighted a woman's (hitsch2010qme). In a Tinder field experiment, women swiped right on a master's degree much more than on a bachelor's, while men showed no preference and no aversion to highly educated women (neyt2019). In speed dating, women weighted intelligence more, men weighted looks more, and men did not value a woman's intelligence or ambition beyond their own (fisman2006).

So: a sex difference in status weight is visible when people screen profiles, but much weaker once people interact. Both facts are needed for an honest model.

## 5. Actual matching

### 5.1 Education

The strongest pattern in marriage data is **like marries like** (homogamy), not women marrying up. US educational homogamy rose from 1960 to 2003 (schwartz2005), and assortative matching runs through all of partner choice (kalmijn1998).

Educational hypergamy did exist: for most of the 20th century, when husband and wife differed, he usually had more education. This has **reversed**. Census data from 56 countries showed a near-universal decline (esteve2012); data from 120 countries (1960 to 2011) tie the end of hypergamy to women overtaking men in education (esteve2016). In Western countries, couples where the wife has more education now outnumber those where the husband has more (vanbavel2018). In the US, educational assortative marriage reversed from women marrying up (1980) to women marrying down (2008 to 2012) (qian2017). In Europe, highly educated women increasingly partnered with less-educated men rather than stay single, while low-educated men more often stayed single (dehauw2017).

Does that mean women "stopped preferring" to marry up? An agent-based model of 12 European countries reproduced both the old hypergamy and the new hypogamy **without** any preference for marrying up and **without** preferences changing: the supply of educated men and women did the work (grow2015). That does not prove there is no preference, but it shows the marriage pattern alone cannot prove one.

Hypogamy is not a recipe for divorce: US marriages where the wife had more education were once more likely to end, but this disappeared in recent cohorts (schwartz2014).

Where norms and supply clash, marriage can fall instead: in Japan, the decline in marriage among highly educated women is linked to a shortage of men with the expected education and earnings (raymo2005).

### 5.2 Income

Income hypergamy has outlived educational hypergamy. In the US, the tendency for women to marry men with higher incomes persisted between 1980 and 2008 to 2012, and was **stronger** when the wife was as educated as or more educated than the husband (qian2017). In Sweden, women in education-hypogamous couples had higher class background and occupational prestige but lower income than their partners, partly because lower-earning people select into those unions (chudnovskaya2020). "Up" on one dimension is often "down" on another.

At the same time, spouses' earnings have become more similar to each other over time, which again is like-with-like (schwartz2010).

**Structural shortage.** Unmarried US women's "expected" husbands (built from husbands of comparable married women) would earn about 58% more than the unmarried men actually available, and be more often employed (90% vs 70%) (lichter2020). Local manufacturing decline lowered young men's relative earnings and reduced marriage (autor2019). Both fit a provider expectation, but they are about the market, not just about preferences.

**"Women outearning husbands".** Bertrand, Kamenica and Pan (2015) found a sharp drop in the number of US couples just past the point where the wife earns more, plus lower marriage where women were likely to out-earn men, and read this as a male-breadwinner norm. Later work challenges the reading: simple matching models without any norm reproduce the distribution, and the drop is caused by couples with exactly equal earnings (binder2022); in Finland the drop comes from co-working spouses whose earnings converge, and goes with women's relative earnings rising (zinovyeva2021); a 2024 replication found the labour-force result disappears with hourly wages and predicted earnings (rosenberg2024, working paper). The norm may exist, but this particular evidence does not settle it.

### 5.3 Age

Age hypergamy is the most stable finding. Women prefer and marry slightly older men at all ages, while men's preference for younger women grows with age (kenrick1992). In the 45-country data, women's partners were on average 2.4 years older and men's 2.3 years younger, and the gap was smaller in more gender-equal countries, the one place where gender equality clearly moderated a sex difference (walter2020). Men consider a wider age range as they age, but their interest in much younger women is rarely turned into actual partnering, because the other side also chooses (antfolk2017).

### 5.4 Online dating

Online dating is now the most common way US heterosexual couples meet (rosenfeld2019), so app behaviour matters.

- **Everyone aims up.** On a large US site, desirability formed one shared hierarchy, and **both** men and women messaged people about 25% more desirable than themselves (bruch2018). Both sexes message the most desirable people regardless of their own standing; women send about four times fewer messages, but their messages are twice as likely to get a reply (kreager2014).
- **Asymmetries by sex.** Men's desirability rose with education and peaked near age 50; women's fell from age 18 and peaked with an undergraduate degree (bruch2018). Men get far fewer replies than women.
- **Final pairs are assortative.** Couple similarity arises mostly because messages "up" go unanswered (kreager2014). Estimated preferences fed into a Gale-Shapley matching reproduce actual site matches and marriage sorting (hitsch2010aer).
- **Screening.** Users apply a few hard cut-offs before weighing other traits (bruch2016).
- **Swipe apps.** On Tinder, men liked many profiles and matched about 0.6% of the time, women matched about 10% (tyson2016, a small conference study). Popular "Gini of likes" numbers (0.58 on Tinder; 0.54 or 0.73 for men on Hinge, depending on the retelling) come from an anonymous blog and from company figures reported in the press; they cannot be checked.

**The 80/20 claim.** The online claim says most women chase a small top group of men. The peer-reviewed data show something different: attention is unequal for both sexes, men get fewer replies, but women's messages are not limited to a top 20%; both sexes aim about the same amount above themselves; and actual couples are mostly matched. OkCupid's own 2009 data, often quoted as "women rate 80% of men below average", also showed that women's messaging was only slightly skewed toward attractive men (rudder2009). Attention inequality on an app is not the same as partnering inequality in a population.

### 5.5 Same-sex couples as a comparison

If status-seeking were only about "women choosing men", it should not appear among same-sex couples. The evidence is thin. Some sex differences in preferences, notably men's greater weight on looks, appear across orientation (bailey1994, lippa2007), which points to sex-linked rather than purely role-linked preferences. On the other hand, same-sex couples are less alike on race, age and education than different-sex married couples (schwartz2009samesex). There is little direct work on status "up" in same-sex couples. This is a gap the project could address with census or survey data.

### 5.6 Status vs attractiveness

The classic story is that women trade beauty for men's status. In a representative sample of 1,507 US couples, controlling for the fact that partners match on each trait removed nearly all evidence of such exchange (mcclintock2014). A comment argued the models were mis-specified and that the effect of women's beauty on partner status is large (gullickson2017); the reply maintained the original conclusion (mcclintock2017). Verdict: unclear, and a clear lesson that matching can look like exchange.

## 6. Explanations

| Account | Core idea | What it predicts | Where it fits the data | Where it struggles |
|---------|-----------|------------------|------------------------|--------------------|
| **Evolutionary** (buss1989, kenrick1992, gangestad2006) | Different reproductive costs shaped different preferences: women value resources and ambition, men value youth and looks | Sex differences in every culture; age gaps that widen for older men | Universality of the stated difference and of age gaps (walter2020) | Small revealed differences once people meet (eastwick2014); fast reversal of educational hypergamy |
| **Social role / biosocial** (eagly1999, wood2002) | The division of labour shapes preferences; when women earn their own resources, the provider preference weakens | Smaller sex differences in more equal societies | Some cross-national data (kasser1999, zentner2012); shrinking age gaps (walter2020) | Largest replications find little moderation (zhang2019, walter2020) |
| **Structural / market** (kalmijn1998, grow2015, lichter2020) | Who marries whom depends heavily on who is available and who meets whom (schools, work, apps) | Patterns follow supply: hypergamy flips when women out-educate men | Global end of educational hypergamy (esteve2016); agent-based fit without preference change (grow2015) | Does not explain why the stated sex difference exists in the first place |

These accounts are not exclusive. A reasonable reading: a modest, cross-cultural sex difference in stated preferences exists; its size may depend on context; and actual pairing is mostly driven by like-with-like matching and by supply.

## 7. Methodological pitfalls

1. **Stated vs revealed.** Ratings of an ideal partner predict real attraction weakly (eastwick2014). Do not infer behaviour from surveys or preferences from behaviour without a model.
2. **Composition (supply) effects.** If more women than men have degrees, many women must partner with less-educated men or stay single. Raw shares of "up" and "down" mix preference with supply. Log-linear models or simulations that hold margins fixed are needed (schwartz2005, qian2017, grow2015).
3. **Assortative mating vs hypergamy.** A tendency to match like with like plus a gender gap in a trait (e.g., pay) produces "hypergamy" in that trait with no wish to marry up. Matching can also mimic exchange (mcclintock2014).
4. **Selection.** Couples are not a random sample: who marries, who stays single and who divorces all depend on the traits studied (chudnovskaya2020). Dating-site users are self-selected; fake-profile experiments test first impressions only.
5. **Measuring "up".** Education, income, occupation, class and age can point in different directions. Income is measured after marriage and is affected by it (specialisation, childbirth). Use pre-marriage or predicted measures where possible (rosenberg2024).
6. **Distribution artefacts.** Bunching at exactly equal earnings created the famous breadwinner discontinuity (binder2022).
7. **Country-level correlations.** Neighbouring countries share culture (Galton's problem); results change with controls (gangestad2006, zhang2019).
8. **Effect-size framing.** Multivariate distances grow with the number of items; single-trait differences are small. Report both.
9. **Grey data.** Most swipe-app inequality figures are company or anonymous numbers that cannot be checked. Treat them as hypotheses.
10. **Attention vs outcomes.** Messages and likes measure attention; marriage and cohabitation measure outcomes. The 80/20 claim usually confuses the two.

## 8. What a model or platform could do honestly

**Goal:** let people play with the mechanisms and see where hypergamy appears, partly appears or disappears, using population-level parameters from the literature.

**Option A: two-sided matching market (Gale-Shapley).** Simulate a population with traits (education, income, age, attractiveness score), give each agent a preference score for candidates, and run deferred acceptance (gale1962). This has precedent: hitsch2010aer used estimated preferences plus Gale-Shapley to reproduce real matches. Outputs: share of hypergamous, homogamous and hypogamous couples by trait, share single by group.

**Option B: Euclidean mate-value model.** Score candidates by distance between a chooser's ideal and the candidate's traits; this beat seven alternatives across 45 countries (conroybeam2018, conroybeam2019sr).

**Option C: agent-based search with ageing and meeting places** (grow2015). More realistic, more work; good for the "history slider" (1960 vs 2020 education supply).

**Two-stage choice.** Screen with a few deal-breakers, then rank (bruch2016). Add "aspiration": agents contact people about 25% above themselves and reply rates fall with the gap (bruch2018).

**Parameters from the literature (population averages, with uncertainty):**

| Parameter | Value or range | Source |
|-----------|----------------|--------|
| Sex difference in weight on financial prospects (stated) | about 0.30 points on a 1-7 scale; varies by country | walter2020 |
| Sex difference in weight on attractiveness (stated) | about 0.27 points | walter2020 |
| Revealed effect of earning prospects on attraction | r about .10, both sexes | eastwick2014 |
| Revealed effect of attractiveness | r about .40, both sexes | eastwick2014 |
| Preferred / actual age gap | women's partners about +2.4 years, men's about -2.3 years | walter2020 |
| Aspiration in messaging | about 25% above own desirability, both sexes | bruch2018 |
| Education supply | from census, user-adjustable (e.g., women's share of degrees) | esteve2016 |

**Honesty rules for the model:**
- Run each scenario with a **"no hypergamy preference"** baseline (preference for similarity only) and show how much of the outcome supply alone produces. This is the most informative single comparison.
- Show **ranges**, not single numbers: vary parameters across the literature's range and across countries.
- Label stated-preference parameters vs revealed-preference parameters, and let users switch between them.
- Separate **attention** outputs (likes, messages) from **outcome** outputs (couples formed).
- Publish code and parameter sources; mark which parameters are guesses.

## 9. Ethics and framing

A "your life is over" or "your market value is X" output would be both inaccurate and harmful.

- **Inaccurate determinism.** All parameters are group averages with large overlap between the sexes and large individual variation; applying them to one person is an ecological fallacy. Revealed weights are small (eastwick2014), and couples are mostly assortative, so a low score on one trait says little about a person's chances.
- **Stigma and fatalism.** Ranking people's "mate value" reinforces the fatalistic narratives the online "80/20" discourse already spreads, and can feed hopelessness. Labelling groups of women as "hypergamous" or men as "low value" invites hostility.
- **Sensitive traits.** Dating data show racial hierarchies in attention (bruch2018, hitsch2010qme). A platform should not output race-based desirability for individuals.
- **Privacy.** If users enter their own traits, store nothing by default; never build profiles.
- **Self-fulfilling effects.** Telling someone they are unlikely to partner can change behaviour (withdrawal), which then "confirms" the prediction.

**Safer fun alternatives:**
- **Myth or data quiz:** show a viral claim, let users guess, then reveal what studies show, with the verdict colour from section 2.
- **Market sandbox:** sliders for education supply, preference weights and aspiration; watch hypergamy flip to hypogamy as women's education rises. Population-level only.
- **Then vs now:** compare 1960 and 2020 couple patterns from census data.
- **"Same data, different story":** show the same marriage table read as preference vs as supply.
- **Your guess vs the data:** users predict the share of wives more educated than husbands; the platform shows the real figure for their country.

## 10. Open questions

1. How much of today's income hypergamy is preference, pay gap, or selection? Designs using predicted pre-marriage earnings could separate them.
2. Does the stated sex difference in status preference shrink with gender equality? The largest replications disagree with earlier work.
3. How unequal is attention on swipe apps, measured on audited, representative data rather than company blogs? And does attention inequality translate into partnering inequality?
4. Do same-sex couples show "status up" patterns, and in which traits?
5. Does educational hypogamy change who earns more over the life course?
6. How do results outside Western, educated, industrialised countries (e.g., South and East Asia, where norms differ) compare? raymo2005 suggests mismatch can lower marriage rather than flip hypergamy.
7. Do men's preferences (e.g., avoiding women who out-earn or out-rank them, fisman2006) explain part of what is called female hypergamy?

## 11. Beyond status: what else people say and do

Sections 3 to 5 are about status. People argue about much more: casual vs serious dating, height, build, penis size, intelligence, looks, age, race, "bad boys" and paternity. This section reports what the research shows on each, with the same verdict scale (rows marked *Beyond status* in the table of section 2). Attractiveness vs status trade-offs are in section 5.6 and age gaps in couples in section 5.3; they are not repeated here.

> [!warning] Read the samples
> Much of this evidence comes from small groups of Western university students rating photos, figures or questionnaires. That measures *stated* or *first-impression* preferences. Where data on real contacts or couples exist, they are reported separately, and they usually show smaller effects.

### 11.1 Short-term vs long-term mating

**The theory.** Sexual strategies theory says men and women have separate strategies for short-term (casual) and long-term (committed) relationships, and that what they want in a partner shifts with the context (buss1993, buss2019).

**What the studies show.**
- **Minimum standards.** 93 US students set their minimum acceptable level on 24 traits for four levels of commitment, from a single date to marriage. Women were more selective overall, especially on status-related traits. Men accepted much lower standards for a sexual partner but were nearly as selective as women for a long-term partner (kenrick1990).
- **Necessities vs luxuries.** In mate-budget tasks, women spent first on status for a long-term partner (li2002, section 3), but for a short-term partner both sexes spent first on physical attractiveness (likenrick2006).
- **Bodies and context.** Body shape mattered most for a one-time sexual encounter and least for a serious relationship, for both sexes (braun2006). Women's recent casual partners were more muscular than their other partners (frederick2007).
- **Real meetings.** In a German community speed-dating study (N = 382, ages 18 to 54), both sexes chose mainly on looks, and women additionally on men's education and income. Only 6% of matches led to sex and 4% to a relationship within a year (asendorpf2011).

**The ovulation claim.** A popular claim is that women prefer more masculine or "high-quality" men around ovulation, especially as short-term partners. Two meta-analyses of the same literature disagree: one found such shifts for short-term attractiveness (gildersleeve2014), the other found them "largely nonsupportive", limited to loose definitions of the fertile phase and to published studies (wood2014). The largest longitudinal study (N = 584, hormone measures) found no link between hormones and preference for masculine faces (jones2018), and a preregistered study with ovulation tests (N = 157) found no shift in preferences for men's behaviour (stern2020). Both large studies still found that masculinity counts more in short-term than long-term ratings. Verdict: the context effect holds, the cycle effect mostly does not.

**What this means for hypergamy.** The stated preference for status is mainly a preference for a *long-term* partner. In short-term contexts the sexes look more alike: both put looks first. A model that applies the same status weight to every kind of relationship overstates hypergamy for casual dating.

### 11.2 Height (both sexes)

**Stated.** Women want a taller partner more strongly and more consistently than men want a shorter one. In a Dutch student sample (N = 650), women were most satisfied with a partner about 21 cm taller, men with a partner about 8 cm shorter (stulp2013paid).

**First contact online.** On a US dating site, women were more likely to contact men taller than themselves (marginal effects .050 for 2 to 5 inches taller, .071 for more than 5 inches), men were more likely to contact women shorter than themselves, and neither sex showed interest in a partner who would break the norm. The preferences were almost entirely *relative to one's own height*; women showed no preference for absolute height (hitsch2010qme). The widely shared claim that men of 6'3" get 65% more messages than men of 5'7" comes from a 2006 draft of that work; the published papers say non-robust earlier results were dropped. A study of a large Chinese site found that some apparent attribute preferences in messages, height among them, were close to what random selection would produce (xia2014, preprint). Self-reported heights on profiles are also inflated, especially by men (hancock2007).

**Actual couples.** In 12,502 UK couples, partners' heights correlate at r = .18. The man was not taller in 7.5% of couples, against 10.2% expected if people paired at random; couples with the man more than 25 cm taller were also slightly rarer than chance (13.9% vs 15.7%). The authors conclude that height preferences translate into pairing "only modestly" (stulp2013plos). The meta-analytic partner correlation for height is r = .24 (horwitz2023). So the male-taller norm exists, but random pairing would already produce it about 90% of the time, simply because men are taller on average.

**Women's height.** Men's preference about women's height is weaker; men mostly avoid women taller than themselves (hitsch2010qme, stulp2013paid). Evidence on how height affects women's own partnering is thinner; the review by stulp2016 notes that links between height and mating success remain uncertain for both sexes.

### 11.3 Body build and frame (men)

- **Upper-body strength.** Raters judged either the strength or the attractiveness of men in photos. Perceived strength explained over 70% of the variation in bodily attractiveness; with height and leanness, about 80%. The strongest men were rated most attractive, with no drop-off (sell2017).
- **Muscularity.** Across six studies, women rated muscular men as sexier but also as less committed, and moderate muscularity was rated most attractive (frederick2007). This partly conflicts with sell2017 on whether "more" is always better.
- **Shoulders and torso.** A man's waist-to-shoulder ratio raised his desirability, most for a one-time encounter (braun2006). With life-size computer figures, torso shape (shoulder-to-hip ratio) was the strongest predictor of attractiveness, ahead of height and penis size (mautz2013).
- **Arm span ("wingspan").** No study on arm span and attractiveness or partner choice was found (searches in Crossref and web search, 2026-10-28). Arm span is closely tied to height, so most of any effect would be a height effect. The nearest proxy is leg length: legs about 5% longer than average were rated most attractive, and much longer legs were rated lower (sorokowski2008).

Limits: these are ratings of photos and figures, mostly by students, not choices of real partners.

### 11.4 Penis size

- **Lab ratings.** Women rated life-size projected figures that varied in height, torso shape and flaccid penis size. Larger size raised attractiveness, but with diminishing returns; its effect was about as large as height's, and it mattered more in taller men and men with a more masculine torso (mautz2013). Torso shape mattered most.
- **3D models.** 75 US women chose among 33 3D models. They preferred about 16.3 cm length for a one-time partner and 16.0 cm for a long-term partner (circumference 12.7 vs 12.2 cm), which the authors call "only slightly larger than average" (prause2015). The short-term vs long-term difference is tiny, and comparing handled models with clinical measurements is not straightforward.
- **Actual sizes.** Across clinician-measured studies of up to 15,521 men, mean erect length is 13.1 cm (SD 1.7) and circumference 11.7 cm (SD 1.1); length correlates with height (r = .2 to .6) (veale2015). Most men are close to average.
- **Satisfaction.** In an online survey of 52,031 heterosexual US adults, 85% of women were satisfied with their partner's size, while only 55% of men were satisfied with their own and 45% wanted to be larger (lever2006).

Summary: a real but small factor in lab ratings, smaller than torso shape, and much less important to women than men tend to believe. No study links it to actual partner choice.

### 11.5 Intelligence

**Stated.** Intelligence ranks near the top for both sexes, together with kindness, above earnings and looks (buss1989, walter2020). In speed dating, women weighted a partner's intelligence more than men did, and men did not value a woman's intelligence beyond their own level (fisman2006).

**A ceiling?** When people rate how desirable a partner at each IQ percentile would be, desirability peaked around the 90th percentile (about IQ 120) and dropped slightly at the 99th (gignac2018, N = 383). A replication with 214 young adults found the same peak and drop for IQ and easygoingness, but not for kindness or looks; people's own IQ did not predict their ratings (gignac2019). "Sapiosexuality" (sexual attraction to intelligence) is reported by a minority (gignac2018). These are stated ratings from one lab.

**Actual couples.** Partners are clearly matched on intelligence and education. A meta-analysis of 199 studies found partner correlations of r = .44 for IQ and r = .55 for education; in 79,074 UK Biobank couples, education correlated at .48 and a brief fluid-intelligence test at .23 (horwitz2023). This is like-with-like matching, the same pattern as for status in section 5.

**Does intelligence help people find a partner?** Evidence is thin and old. In a Scottish cohort born in 1921 (N = 883), each standard deviation of childhood IQ *lowered* women's odds of marrying by mid-life (odds ratio 0.42), while men's association was positive but not significant (taylor2005). That cohort lived under very different norms; we found no modern population study that settles the question.

### 11.6 Looks, low attractiveness and age on dating sites

This covers women as well as men as targets.

- **Looks drive attention for both sexes.** On a US site, a man was 10.7% more likely to contact a woman in the 81st to 90th looks percentile than one in the lowest decile; for women contacting men the figure was 7.9%. Men's looks preferences were stronger, but the difference was modest (hitsch2010qme). In face-to-face studies, attractiveness predicts romantic interest at about r = .40 for both sexes (eastwick2014, section 4).
- **The OkCupid charts (blog data, grade D).** Men rated women's looks in a symmetric curve centred on "medium", yet two-thirds of men's messages went to the best-looking third of women. Women rated about 80% of men as below medium, yet messaged men only slightly above the middle. The most attractive men received about 11 times as many messages as the lowest-rated (rudder2009). These numbers are from one site and were never peer reviewed, but their direction agrees with hitsch2010qme.
- **Low attractiveness.** On HOTorNOT, people rated less attractive chose less attractive dates but did not fool themselves about their dates' looks; men's choices depended less on their own looks than women's (lee2008). On a US site, couples end up similar mostly because messages "up" go unanswered (kreager2014).
- **Age.** Men on a US site disliked contacting older women (marginal effects -.054 for women 5 to 9 years older, -.121 for 10 or more) and showed a slight preference for somewhat younger women; women preferred men of their own age or a few years older (hitsch2010qme). In German online-dating data, men's preference for younger women grew as they aged, while women's preferences became more varied (skopek2011age). Women's measured desirability on a US site was highest at 18 and fell with age, men's peaked near 50 (bruch2018). Men state wider and younger ranges than they actually end up with (antfolk2017, section 5.3).

### 11.8 Ethnicity and race: documented bias

This sub-section describes discrimination patterns. It does not, and should not be read to, rank anyone's worth or "market value".

- **Same-race preference is the strongest pattern.** On US dating sites, both sexes mostly contact people of their own race; 30 of 31 estimated race effects were negative, and women's same-race preferences were stronger than men's (hitsch2010qme). Racial homophily dominated searching on another large site (lin2013).
- **A racial hierarchy in replies.** Women replied only to men of similar or more "dominant" racial status, and non-black men replied to all but black women; a college degree did not offset racial preferences among white users (lin2013). In 6,070 US profiles, white daters were least open to dating across race, and exclusion was gendered: Asian men and black women were the most excluded (robnett2011). In messaging data, a white man was 10% less likely to contact a black woman than a white woman, and a white woman 12% less likely to contact an Asian man than a white man (hitsch2010qme). City dating markets split into submarkets by age and ethnicity (bruch2019). A book-length synthesis calls this "digital-sexual racism" (curington2021).
- **OkCupid's charts (grade D)** showed the same direction and, by the company's own account, no decline from 2009 to 2014 (rudder2014race).
- **Actual marriages.** Homogamy by race remains strong, but US black-white intermarriage tripled between 1980 and 2008, while intermarriage with whites stalled for Asians and American Indians (qian2011). Online, race preferences can be expressed through filters; offline, opportunity (who you meet) matters a lot.
- **Platform changes.** Some apps removed ethnicity filters in 2020; Grindr announced the removal on 1 June 2020 after criticism during the Black Lives Matter protests (press reports, grade D; see notes). There is no published evaluation of whether removing filters changed behaviour.

**Why the "red flags" input is shaped this way.** The short-term bonus is real but small and mostly carried by looks and confidence (jauk2016, dufner2013), and high scorers do report more partners (borrazleon2021); the largest controlled study of written profiles found no advantage once faces were randomised (dragostinov2025). The long-term penalty reflects what people say they want for a partner (jonason2015birds) and what happens in couples: more infidelity and lower satisfaction (ataman2025, kardum2024). The app should show a note such as "Red flags win the first date, not the marriage. This is a joke, not a strategy." It must never suggest that someone should act more manipulative or callous.

**Why ethnicity is excluded.** The studies in 11.8 document discrimination: people of some groups are contacted and answered less because of their race. Putting race into a score would turn that bias into a "result" and present it as a fact about individuals, reproducing the discrimination the studies describe. If the app discusses race at all, it should be in the "myth or data" quiz as a documented bias, with no personal output.

### 11.10 The Dark Triad: do "bad boys" win?

**What is measured.** The Dark Triad is three overlapping personality traits: narcissism (grandiosity, entitlement), Machiavellianism (cynical manipulation) and psychopathy (callousness, impulsivity) (paulhus2002). The Dark Tetrad adds everyday sadism (buckels2013). Every study below measures *subclinical* levels with short questionnaires in ordinary samples, mostly students and online panels. A "high scorer" is someone in the upper part of a normal distribution, not a clinical psychopath. Most data are self-reports and cross-sectional (muris2017). "Bad boy" in the online debate usually means a mix of confidence, dominance, risk-taking and low agreeableness; the studies below test those pieces separately, and the answer differs by piece.

**Sex differences.** Men score higher on all three traits on average (muris2017). For narcissism the gap is d = .26 across 470,846 people, driven by entitlement and leadership facets, with almost no gap in exhibitionism or vulnerable narcissism (grijalva2015). Across 49 countries the narcissism gap is larger in more developed countries (jonason2020countries). These are modest differences: many women score higher than the average man.

**Partner counts and casual sex: the most solid finding.** This is where the "bad boys win" claim is strongest, and it should be said plainly. In 224 US students, the traits correlated with short-term mating (casual sex, more partners, unrestricted sociosexuality) but not with long-term mating, more strongly in men, and partly explained why men report more short-term mating (jonason2009). The narcissism and short-term link held in all 11 world regions of a 30,470-person survey (schmitt2017narc). In two student samples the Dark Triad, and low Honesty-Humility, strongly predicted short-term mating tendencies and sexual quid pro quos (lee2013sex). In a mixed-sex sample of young adults, narcissism and psychopathy, but not Machiavellianism, correlated with number of sexual partners in men and in women analysed separately, and narcissism also predicted higher self-rated attractiveness and mate value (borrazleon2021). In budget tasks, high scorers prefer one-night stands, friends with benefits and "booty calls" over serious relationships (jonason2012choices, jonason2011mate). The pattern is not men-only: in a 2023 replication (N = 495), Machiavellianism carried it in men and psychopathy in women, and psychopathic women reported more partners, unprotected sex and one-night stands (freyth2023). On Tinder, psychopathy goes with using the app for sex (lyons2022tinder). Caveats: partner counts are self-reported and narcissists over-claim; more partners is not the same as more children. We found no large study showing that high-scoring men have more children today. One study found that women who preferred narcissistic-looking male faces had more children, after controlling for age, sociosexuality and health, while a preference for Machiavellian faces went with fewer (marcinkowska2016, single correlational study).

| *Real meeting.* Speed dating, 90 adults, 691 dates | Narcissism predicted being chosen and rated desirable, both sexes; psychopathy predicted short-term appeal in women; explained by looks in women and extraversion in men; nothing "dark" left after controlling looks | jauk2016 |

Read together: there is a real and replicated advantage for **narcissism** in first meetings and short-term mating, in both sexes. It shows up in what people actually do (who gets a phone number, who is chosen at speed dating), not only in what they say. It is largely a package of looking good, grooming, boldness and extraversion, so it is fair to say women respond to confident self-promotion, and less fair to say they respond to the callous core of the traits. For **Machiavellianism and psychopathy** the picture is weaker: one vignette study found Machiavellian "cads" preferred for flings (aitken2013), but faces high on the traits are disliked for both short and long term (brewer2018faces, lyons2016faces), and the largest randomised profile study found low levels preferred (dragostinov2025). The famous vignette effect (carter2014) comes from one small group of young students. People who score high on the traits themselves, or who are more open to casual sex, like these traits more in partners (jonason2015birds, watts2019, marcinkowska2015), and couples resemble each other on all three traits (richards2024).

**Dominance.** In four experiments, dominant behaviour raised men's sexual attractiveness but not women's, and only when it was dominance, not aggressiveness or domineering (sadalla1987). Later work narrowed this. In three studies (N = 348), dominance alone did not increase women's attraction; prosocial men were rated more attractive, and dominance helped only in combination with kindness (jensencampbell1995). When a neutral control description was added, both dominant and non-dominant descriptions lowered desirability compared with the control, so the earlier "dominance wins" result partly reflected an unattractive non-dominant condition (burger1999). Women preferred prestige over dominance, and preferred high dominance only when it was shown in athletic competition and only for a short-term partner (snyder2008). Verdict: **partly**. Assertiveness and confidence help; dominance over others does not, except in narrow contexts.

**Do nice guys finish last?** It depends on the scoreboard. In *partner counts*, the less agreeable and more narcissistic or psychopathic report more partners (jonason2009, borrazleon2021), most women agree that nice guys have fewer partners, and women with more partners pick the bad boy more (herold1999); for casual sex, looks counted for more than niceness, and the authors concluded that their overall results "did not favor the nice guy stereotype" (urbaniak2003, secondary). As *dates and long-term partners*, nice men are preferred once looks are similar (herold1999, jensencampbell1995, mcdaniel2005). So "nice guys finish last" is roughly true for the number of sexual partners and false for being chosen as a partner. A 2006 follow-up by the same authors (urbaniak2006) was not read.

**Does it fade?** For narcissism, yes, at least among strangers. In groups meeting weekly, self-enhancers made a good first impression and were rated negatively by the seventh meeting (paulhus1998). In 311 people over three weeks, the narcissist's popularity fell as the charming "admiration" side lost influence and the arrogant "rivalry" side gained it (leckelt2015). No study followed dating couples in the same way.

**Long-term outcomes.** High scorers poach other people's partners more, are poached more, and leave partners for someone new more often (jonason2010poach). A meta-analysis of 11 studies found infidelity correlates with narcissism r = .13, Machiavellianism r = .23 and psychopathy r = .25 (ataman2025). The same holds for women: women's Dark Triad scores predicted their past infidelity and their revenge after a partner's infidelity (brewer2015). In 205 couples, both partners' dark traits lowered both partners' satisfaction, and mismatched couples did worse (kardum2024). Effects are small to moderate and mostly self-reported. That narcissistic traits still attract women who want marriage (haslam2015) is the uncomfortable part: the authors call it a testament to how well the narcissistic personality works for short-term mating.

**Sadism.** We found no study on sadism and attractiveness or partner choice. In the one dating-app study that measured it, sadism had no headline effect (lyons2022tinder).

**Verdict by sub-claim.**

| Sub-claim | Verdict |
|-----------|---------|
| High scorers (narcissism, psychopathy) have more casual sex and more partners | **Exists**, in both sexes, stronger in men (self-report) |
| Narcissistic confidence wins first meetings and short-term interest | **Exists**, in both sexes, mostly via looks and boldness |
| Women rate fictional "dark" men higher | **Mixed**: yes for written narcissistic or Machiavellian "cad" profiles among young women and for flings; no for faces and in the largest randomised study |
| Dominance as such attracts women | **Partly**: confidence yes, dominance over others mostly no |
| Machiavellianism and psychopathy attract most women | **Mostly does not exist**; yes among women high in the traits or open to casual sex |
| Dark men preferred for long-term relationships | **Does not exist** in ratings, though narcissistic traits still attract marriage-minded women |
| Nice guys finish last | **Partly**: in partner counts yes, as partners no |

So the honest summary is **split, not just "partly"**. Confident, good-looking, self-promoting people (often narcissists) really do better in early courtship and in casual sex, and high scorers really do report more partners. Men choosing women show the same pattern (jauk2016, freyth2023). What the data do not show is that women in general prefer callousness or manipulation, or pick dark men as long-term partners.

### 11.11 Paternity certainty and Y-chromosome history

**The online claim.** "10 to 30% of children are not their presumed father's" circulates as proof of a female "dual strategy" (commit to a provider, conceive with someone else).

**What the data show.**
- **Population rates are low.** Y-chromosome typing combined with genealogies puts historical extra-pair paternity at about 1 to 2% per generation in Flanders (larmuseau2013) and 0.9% (95% CI 0.4% to 1.5%) over 330 years among Afrikaners (greeff2015). A nationwide Swedish study of nearly two million families found 1.7%, falling to about 1% today (dahlen2022). These historical estimates are not biased by modern contraception (larmuseau2016).
- **Where the 30% comes from.** A review of 67 studies found nonpaternity of about 2% among men with high paternity confidence, but about 30% among men who doubted and took a test (anderson2006). Another review of 17 estimates gives a range of 0.8% to 30%, median 3.7%, because it mixes test-lab and population samples (bellis2005). The high figures are real, but only for a group selected for suspicion.
- **Context matters.** Rates among married couples in the Low Countries varied from 0.4% to 5.9% over 500 years, peaking among poor families in dense late-19th-century cities (larmuseau2019). Rates are also higher among younger parents, in deprivation and in cohabiting rather than married couples (bellis2005), and among less educated parents in Sweden (dahlen2022).

Realistically, for a man in a Western married couple today the risk is about 1 in 50 to 1 in 100, not 1 in 10. It is not zero, and it is higher in some settings. That low rate also argues against the idea that women routinely "shop around" for other men's genes while staying with a provider (larmuseau2016).

**France.** We found no peer-reviewed estimate of misattributed paternity for the French population: no Y-chromosome genealogy study like the Flemish ones, no register study like the Swedish one, and no published series from French clinical or forensic laboratories. The law is part of the reason. In France a genetic paternity test is legal only when a judge orders it in a filiation case (Civil Code art. 16-11). Carrying out a test outside the legal cases is punishable by up to one year in prison and a €15,000 fine (Penal Code art. 226-28), and requesting one by a €3,750 fine (art. 226-28-1, added in 2011; a 2026 bill proposes to change this, and its status was not checked). So France has no published series of "men who doubted" either. Figures that circulate in French media and on commercial sites (for example about 1,200 court-ordered tests a year against about 20,000 bought abroad) are unsourced and grade D. A 2025 news item in *La Revue du Praticien* summarising the Low Countries work gives no French figure. The nearest data come from next door: about 1 to 2% per generation in Flanders (larmuseau2013) and 0.4% to 5.9% by setting in Belgium and the Netherlands (larmuseau2019). France is likely in a similar range, but that is an inference, not a measurement.

**Did fewer men than women reproduce?** On the genetic evidence, yes. The open question is why.
- **Long run, about 2 to 1.** In Khoisan, Mongolian and Papua New Guinean samples, mtDNA (inherited from mothers) coalesces about twice as deep as the Y chromosome (inherited from fathers), which is best explained by a larger female than male effective population size (wilder2004). In a 456-genome global study, effective sizes estimated from mtDNA were consistently more than twice those from the Y chromosome (karmin2015). Baumeister popularised this as "today's population is descended from twice as many women as men" in his 2007 APA address and 2010 book (baumeister2010). His accompanying "maybe 80% of women but only 40% of men reproduced" is an illustration of what such a ratio could mean, not a measured figure (secondary).
- **The 17 to 1 result is real.** Karmin et al. found a second strong bottleneck in Y-chromosome lineages, not seen in mtDNA, around 8,000 to 4,000 years ago, "when the female Ne is up to 17-fold higher than the male Ne" (karmin2015, full text). So the number comes from the paper itself, not from press coverage. Other large datasets show the same kind of male-specific event: bursts of extreme expansion in male lineages on every continent at times of known migrations and technological innovations (poznik2016), and a continent-wide expansion of a few European patrilineages (I1, R1a and R1b, 64% of the sample) starting about 4,200 to 2,100 years ago (batini2015).
- **What "effective population size" means.** Ne is the size of an idealised population, in which everyone has the same chance of passing on genes, that would lose genetic diversity at the same rate as the real one. A male Ne one seventeenth of the female Ne means that male lines lost diversity as if far fewer men had been contributing. It does not mean that for every man who had children, 17 women did. Male Ne falls when the number of children per man varies a lot (a few men with many children, many with none), but also when whole male lines die out or are replaced together, even if most men in each generation became fathers. The 17 is also a peak value from one method (Bayesian skyline plots) over a period, not a precise constant.
- **Competing explanations.** (1) *Higher variance in male success within groups*: polygyny, wealth and status inherited down the male line, female preference for high-status men, and coercion. Karmin et al. favour culture-driven variance, "a combination of culturally driven increased male variance in offspring number within demes and an increased male-specific variance among demes". (2) *Competition between patrilineal clans*: when men of a clan are related through the father's line and whole clans win or are wiped out, Y diversity collapses even without extreme polygyny; a model shows this reproduces the bottleneck (zeng2018). (3) *Conquest and male-led migration*, which replace local male lines; Karmin et al. cite the Mongol expansions as a historical example, and the European expansions point to Bronze Age social structure (batini2015). (4) *Sex-biased migration and population structure* (for example women moving to their husband's group) can lower the Y-based Ne, though Karmin et al. found simple structure models insufficient on their own. These are not exclusive, and no study can say how much of the signal was female choice.

Verdict: the genetic signal is real, and "17 to 1" is a genuine result when read as an effective-size ratio for that period. Reading it as "women chose one man in seventeen" goes beyond the data; why male lines narrowed is still debated.

**Haplogroup N in Latvia, the Baltics and Finland.** N1c (also written N3 in older papers and N1a1 in current ones; defining marker M46, also called Tat) is one of the commonest male lineages in north-eastern Europe.

In Latvia, three haplogroups (N1c, R1a and I1) cover more than 85% of male lines, and a single R1a branch (R1a-M558) more than a third (pliss2015, 159 men). The populations around the Baltic Sea are genetically similar, with genetic drift increasing the differences in the north (lappalainen2008).

What this means. A lineage carried by 40 to 60% of men today does mean that the men who founded it, and their sons, out-reproduced other male lines over many generations. That is a real reproductive skew between male lineages, not just an accident of sampling. The dating places it thousands of years ago: haplogroup N reached eastern Europe from inner Asia and southern Siberia through serial bottlenecks and later expansions (rootsi2007), and the main northern branches split about 5,000 years ago (95% CI 4,400 to 5,700) and expanded regionally within the last 5,000 years (ilumae2016). That is the same mid-Holocene window as the male bottleneck in karmin2015 and the European patrilineal expansions in batini2015, so the same mix of causes applies: migration, founder effects, drift in small northern populations, and male-line success. What it does not show is that a few living men father most children today. A frequency built up over 150 or more generations says nothing about how children are spread among men in the current generation, and it is not specific evidence of hypergamy, because female choice is only one of the candidate mechanisms.
