# Annotated bibliography: is hypergamy real?

One entry per source, three lines each: (1) claim, method and sample, (2) finding, (3) relevance. Keys match `bibliography.bib`. Every citation was checked on 2026-10-28 against Crossref, Semantic Scholar, RePEc or Open Library. Numbers marked *(abstract)* come from the abstract; numbers marked *(full text)* were read in the paper; *(secondary)* means taken from a summary and not checked in the original.

> [!warning] Evidence grades used below
> **A** = peer-reviewed, large sample or meta-analysis, replicated. **B** = peer-reviewed, single study or contested. **C** = conference paper, working paper or book. **D** = company blog or anonymous post (cited only to trace where an online claim came from).

## Stated preferences

**buss1989** (A). Survey of mate preferences, 37 samples in 33 countries, N = 10,047, with marriage-age records for 27 countries as a check.
Women rated "good financial prospects" and ambition higher than men did in most samples; men rated youth and looks higher; actual age at marriage matched the stated age preferences *(abstract)*.
The founding data set for "women prefer status/resources" as a stated preference (definition 2). Says nothing about who actually marries whom.

**walter2020** (A). Pre-planned replication of Buss 1989 in 45 countries, N = 14,399, multilevel models, plus actual partner ages.
Women preferred good financial prospects more than men (b = -0.30 on a 1-7 scale); men preferred physical attractiveness more (b = 0.27); kindness, intelligence and health were rated higher than both by both sexes; multivariate D = 0.73 on five items; women's partners were 2.4 years older, men's 2.3 years younger; gender equality predicted smaller age gaps but did not robustly predict other sex differences *(full text)*.
The best current estimate of the size of the stated sex difference: real, cross-cultural, but small on any single item and outranked by kindness and intelligence.

**conroybeam2015** (A). Re-analysis of the Buss 37-culture data (n = 10,153) with a multivariate distance (Mahalanobis D) over all 18 preference items.
Overall D = 2.41, about 22.8% overlap; sex predicted from preferences with 92.2% accuracy; the multivariate difference was smaller in more gender-equal cultures but present everywhere *(abstract)*.
Shows why "small per item" and "large overall" can both be true; important for honest reporting of effect sizes. Multivariate D is sensitive to the number and correlation of items.

**zentner2012** (B). Two cross-national samples (10 countries, N = 3,177; 31 countries, N = 8,953) related to the Global Gender Gap Index.
Sex differences in preferences (including for a provider/older partner) were largest in gender-unequal countries and smallest in gender-equal ones *(secondary: press release)*.
Main evidence for the social role prediction. Later larger replications did not confirm it beyond age (see zhang2019, walter2020).

**zhang2019** (B). Pre-registered-style replication, 36 countries, N = 3,073, open data, tests Galton's problem.
Women preferred good earning capacity more, men preferred attractiveness more, but there was little evidence these differences shrink with gender equality; one suggestive result vanished after controls *(abstract)*.
Counterweight to zentner2012; the gender-equality moderation of preferences is unresolved.

**kasser1999** (B). Re-analysis of the Buss 37-culture data against reproductive freedom and educational equality.
Women's (not men's) preference for resource-acquisition traits was strongest in cultures low in both female reproductive freedom and educational equality *(abstract)*.
Early structural evidence that the provider preference tracks women's own access to resources.

**eagly1999** (B). Theory paper with re-analysis of the Buss data against UN gender empowerment measures.
Argued that sex differences in mate preferences arise from the division of labour (social role theory) and shrink where gender roles are more equal *(secondary)*.
Core statement of the social role / biosocial explanation; the main rival to the evolutionary account.

**wood2002** (B). Cross-cultural review of sex-typed behaviour in Psychological Bulletin.
Proposed a biosocial model: physical sex differences interact with economy and social structure to produce a division of labour, which then shapes psychology *(secondary)*.
The fuller version of the social role account the review contrasts with evolutionary accounts.

**gangestad2006** (B). Target article re-analysing cross-cultural preference data with geographic controls.
Argued that the gender-equality correlations of Eagly and Wood may be an artefact of non-independent neighbouring cultures (Galton's problem) and proposed "evoked culture" *(secondary)*.
Methodological caution for any country-level analysis in this project.

**li2002** (B). Budget-allocation experiments: participants "buy" partner traits with small or large budgets.
With a small budget, women spent first on status/resources and men first on physical attractiveness; with larger budgets the sexes spread spending more evenly *(secondary)*.
Shows that status is treated as a "necessity" by women in a stated-trade-off task; useful for weight priors in a model.

## Stated vs revealed preferences

**eastwick2008** (B). Speed-dating study comparing stated ideals with actual romantic interest after real meetings.
Stated sex differences appeared (women said earning prospects matter more), but they did not predict who people actually liked after meeting face to face *(secondary)*.
Key example of the gap between stated preferences (definition 2) and revealed choices (definition 3).

**eastwick2014** (A). Meta-analysis of 97 studies on whether attractiveness and earning prospects predict romantic evaluations of real people.
Attractiveness predicted evaluations at about r = .40 and earning prospects at about r = .10 for both sexes; sex differences in these correlations were about .03 and non-significant *(secondary, author PDF)*.
Strong evidence that once people meet, the sex difference in how much status drives attraction is small. Contested by Schmitt (2014) and Meltzer et al. (2014) on scope.

**fisman2006** (B). Speed-dating field experiment with random assignment of partners at Columbia University.
Women weighted a partner's intelligence and race more; men weighted attractiveness more; men did not value a woman's intelligence or ambition when it exceeded their own *(secondary)*.
Revealed preferences at first contact; adds the male-side asymmetry (men avoiding women who out-rank them), which also produces "hypergamous" outcomes.

## Same-sex comparisons

**bailey1994** (B). Questionnaire comparing heterosexual and homosexual men and women on mating psychology.
Many sex differences held regardless of orientation, for example men (gay or straight) placing more weight on partner attractiveness *(secondary; detailed status results not verified)*.
Lets the project ask whether a status preference is about "women" or about "partnering with men".

**lippa2007** (B). BBC Internet Survey, over 200,000 participants from many nations, heterosexual and homosexual men and women.
Men rated good looks higher; women rated honesty, humour, kindness and dependability higher; patterns were broadly similar across nations *(secondary: press summary; orientation details not verified)*.
Large-sample comparison across orientation; supports sex-linked rather than purely heterosexual-role-linked preferences, with caveats on self-selected online samples.

**schwartz2009samesex** (B). US Census 1990 and 2000 microdata, log-linear models of partner resemblance.
Same-sex couples are less homogamous than different-sex couples on race/ethnicity, age and education; male couples least, then female couples, cohabitors, married couples *(abstract)*.
Comparison group for actual matching: assortative patterns are weaker without the marriage institution and different-sex roles.

## Educational assortative mating and hypergamy

**kalmijn1998** (A). Review of intermarriage and homogamy research.
Partner choice is shaped by three forces: preferences, third parties (family, group norms) and the structure of the marriage market (who you meet) *(secondary)*.
The general framework the review uses to separate "wanting up" from "meeting up".

**schwartz2005** (A). US Census and CPS 1940-2003, log-linear models of educational homogamy.
Educational homogamy fell 1940-1960, then rose 1960-2003; marrying up became especially rare for the least educated *(abstract)*.
Shows that the dominant pattern is like-with-like, not women-marry-up.

**esteve2012** (A). Census microdata, 56 countries, 1970s-2000s.
Educational hypergamy declined almost everywhere and reversed in some countries; less hypergamy where women's educational advantage is larger *(abstract)*.
Main global evidence that educational hypergamy follows the supply of educated men and women.

**esteve2016** (A). 420 census and survey samples, 120 countries, 1960-2011.
The reversal of the gender gap in education is strongly associated with the end of hypergamy and a rise of hypogamy (wife more educated) *(abstract/secondary)*.
The headline source for "educational hypergamy has ended in most of the world".

**vanbavel2012** (A). Review and data on Europe.
Describes the reversal of the gender gap in education and its consequences for union formation and fertility *(secondary)*.
European context for the trend; earlier statement of the same argument.

**vanbavel2018** (A). Annual Review of Sociology review of Western countries.
Couples where the wife has more education now outnumber those where the husband has more; such marriages are no longer less stable; aversion to female status-dominant unions has declined, though the breadwinner model still shapes some outcomes *(abstract)*.
Best single summary of where the field stands on educational hypogamy.

**dehauw2017** (A). European Social Survey rounds 1-6, many European countries.
As women became more educated, highly educated women more often partnered "down" rather than staying single; medium-educated women partnered "up" less; low-educated men more often stayed single *(secondary)*.
Revealed behaviour: when supply changes, people adapt, and the cost falls more on low-educated men.

**grow2015** (A). Agent-based model of 12 Western European countries, cohorts 1921-2012, calibrated on census and survey data.
Historical hypergamy and its reversal can be reproduced with a preference for similar education plus a preference for higher earnings, without any preference for "marrying up" and without preferences changing over time; supply drives the shift *(full text summary)*.
Strongest evidence that observed hypergamy need not mean a hypergamous preference; a template for the platform model.

**qian2017** (A). US Census 1980 and ACS 2008-2012, newlyweds, log-linear models.
Educational assortative mating reversed from women marrying up to women marrying down, but the tendency for women to marry men with higher incomes persisted, and was stronger when the wife's education equalled or exceeded the husband's *(abstract)*.
The key evidence that income hypergamy outlives educational hypergamy.

**chudnovskaya2020** (A). Swedish register data, couples having a first child together.
In hypogamous couples (she more educated) women had higher class background and occupational prestige but lower income than partners; this reflects selection of lower-earning people into such unions, not only the pay gap *(abstract)*.
"Status" is multidimensional; up on one axis can be down on another.

**schwartz2014** (A). US marriages formed 1950-2004, event-history analysis.
Marriages where the wife had more education used to be more likely to dissolve; this association disappeared in recent cohorts *(abstract)*.
Contradicts the claim that couples where the woman "married down" are doomed.

**raymo2005** (A). Japanese survey data on marriage timing by women's education.
Falling marriage rates of highly educated Japanese women reflect a shortage of men with the education and earnings these women (and norms) expect, a "mismatch" *(secondary)*.
Example of a society where a hypergamy norm plus supply change reduces marriage rather than producing hypogamy.

**lichter2020** (A). ACS 2008-2017, synthetic spouses for unmarried women built from comparable married women's husbands.
The men unmarried women would be expected to marry have about 58% higher income, are more often employed (90% vs 70%) and more often college educated than the unmarried men available *(abstract)*.
Structural shortage of "marriageable" men; explains lower marriage without assuming a new preference.

**autor2019** (A). US local labour markets, trade-shock instrument.
Manufacturing decline reduced young men's earnings relative to women's, lowering marriage and fertility and raising non-marital births *(secondary)*.
Causal evidence that men's economic position shapes marriage rates, consistent with a provider expectation.

## Income and the breadwinner norm

**schwartz2010** (A). US data on spouses' earnings over several decades.
The association between husbands' and wives' earnings rose, contributing to household income inequality *(secondary)*.
Assortative mating on earnings is increasing: "like with like" again, not only "up".

**bertrand2015** (B). US SIPP and administrative data, distribution of the wife's share of couple income.
Sharp drop in the number of couples just past the point where the wife earns more; marriage less likely where women are likely to out-earn men; wives who out-earn do more housework *(secondary)*.
The best-known evidence for a male-breadwinner norm; widely cited online as proof of hypergamy.

**binder2022** (A). Matching models and the same US data.
Simple matching models without any norm reproduce the earnings-gap distribution; the jump at 50% is caused by a point mass of couples with exactly equal earnings *(abstract)*.
Major critique: the famous discontinuity does not identify a norm.

**zinovyeva2021** (A). Finnish linked employer-employee data.
The discontinuity appears because co-working spouses' earnings converge, and it comes with women's relative earnings rising, not falling *(abstract)*.
Second critique of the breadwinner-norm reading.

**rosenberg2024** (C, working paper). Replication of the labour-force analyses in bertrand2015.
Narrow replication succeeds; using hourly wages and predicted earnings turns the cross-sectional effect positive and the fixed-effects effect to a precise zero *(abstract)*.
Further weakens the strongest causal reading; not yet peer reviewed.

## Age

**kenrick1992** (A). Six studies: personal ads, US marriage records, other cultures and historical data.
Women prefer and marry slightly older men at all ages; men's preference for younger women grows as they age *(abstract)*.
Age hypergamy (women partnering with older men) is one of the most stable findings.

**conroybeam2019age** (B). Review and new data on age preferences across cultures.
Argues age preferences influence many mating behaviours; reports cross-cultural data on preferred and actual partner ages *(secondary)*.
Supports age as a dimension where a sex difference exists in both stated and actual matching.

**antfolk2017** (B). Population-based Finnish sample, N = 2,655, ages 18-50.
Women consider a narrower age range and prefer slightly older men; men's considered range widens with age, but interest in much younger women is rarely turned into actual sex *(abstract)*.
Stated vs revealed again: preferences are constrained by the other side's choices.

## Online dating

**hitsch2010aer** (A). US online dating site, about 2003, estimated preferences then simulated Gale-Shapley matching.
Estimated preferences plus stable matching reproduce observed matches and marriage sorting patterns, mostly without search frictions *(abstract)*.
Direct precedent for a Gale-Shapley platform model with estimated weights.

**hitsch2010qme** (A). Same data, contact decisions after viewing profiles.
Strong same-race and similarity preferences; women weight a man's income more than men weight a woman's income; no evidence of strategic shading *(secondary)*.
Revealed (first-contact) evidence for a sex difference in income weight.

**bruch2018** (A). Messaging network on one free US dating site, four cities, January 2014, heterosexual users.
Desirability (PageRank-like) forms a shared hierarchy; men and women both message partners about 25% (26% and 23%) more desirable than themselves; men's desirability rises with education and peaks near age 50, women's falls from 18 and peaks at an undergraduate degree *(full text)*.
Aspiration is two-sided, not a female trait; also shows age and education asymmetries in attention.

**bruch2016** (A). Model of multistage screening on a dating site.
Users apply a few hard cut-offs (deal-breakers) before weighing other traits *(secondary)*.
Suggests a two-stage platform model (screen, then rank) is more realistic than a single weighted score.

**kreager2014** (A). 6 months of messages, mid-sized US city, 8,259 men and 6,274 women.
Both sexes message the most desirable people regardless of own desirability; women send 4 times fewer messages, but female-initiated contacts are twice as likely to connect; couple similarity arises mostly from non-replies *(abstract)*.
Explains how "everyone aims high" coexists with assortative final matches.

**skopek2011** (A). German online dating site, contact and reply behaviour.
Strong educational homophily in contacts; women more often avoid contacting less-educated men *(secondary)*.
Revealed evidence for a residual female-side avoidance of educational "down" in first contact.

**neyt2019** (B). Field experiment on Tinder, 24 fake profiles, 3,600 evaluations, Belgium, ages 23-27.
Women strongly preferred highly educated men; men showed no preference and no aversion to highly educated women; no evidence of educational homogamy preference *(abstract)*.
Clean causal evidence on swiping at the first-screen stage; small and local.

**tyson2016** (C). Tinder activity of fake profiles, conference paper.
Men like a large share of profiles but match about 0.6% of the time; women are selective but match about 10% *(secondary)*.
The best available research source on swipe asymmetry, but tiny and not a full-population study.

**rosenfeld2019** (A). Nationally representative 2017 US survey (How Couples Meet and Stay Together).
Meeting online became the most common way heterosexual US couples meet, overtaking friends around 2013 *(abstract)*.
Why app-based evidence matters for actual matching today.

**rudder2014** (C). Popular book using OkCupid data.
Describes rating and messaging patterns by sex, age and race on OkCupid *(secondary)*.
Source of many viral statistics; useful for context, not for estimates.

**rudder2009** (D). OkCupid company blog post.
Women rated about 80% of men as below medium in looks, but messaged men in a way only slightly skewed toward attractive men *(secondary, archived copy)*.
One root of the "80%" meme; its own data undercut the meme by separating ratings from behaviour.

## Beauty-status exchange

**mcclintock2014** (A). Add Health Romantic Pair Sample, N = 1,507 couples, nationally representative.
Once matching on each trait is controlled, nearly all evidence of women trading beauty for men's status disappears *(abstract)*.
Assortative mating can masquerade as exchange or hypergamy.

**gullickson2017** (B). Comment on mcclintock2014.
Argues McClintock's models are mis-specified and that the total effect of women's beauty on partner status is significant and large *(abstract)*.
The exchange question is open; both readings must be shown.

**mcclintock2017** (B). Reply to gullickson2017.
Maintains that support for beauty-status exchange remains illusory *(title; content secondary)*.
Completes the exchange; the verdict is "unclear".

## Models

**gale1962** (A). Mathematical paper introducing the deferred-acceptance algorithm.
Proves a stable matching always exists in a two-sided market with ranked preferences *(known result)*.
The basic engine for a matching-market toy model.

**conroybeam2018** (B). Agent-based models plus couple data.
A Euclidean model (distance between a person's ideal and a candidate's traits) best explains why high mate-value people get more choice and better preference fulfilment *(abstract)*.
A tested way to turn preference weights into partner scores.

**conroybeam2019sr** (A). 45-country data, n = 14,487, eight agent-based models compared.
Across cultures, people higher in mate value set higher standards and pair with higher-value partners; the Euclidean model fits best *(abstract)*.
Best-validated simple model for a platform; also shows universal assortative pairing.

## Grey literature (not in `bibliography.bib`)

**Anonymous Medium post "Tinder experiments II" (2015)** (D). Self-run experiment, reported Gini 0.58 for likes received by men; critics note very few women observed. URL: https://medium.com/@worstonlinedater/tinder-experiments-ii-guys-unless-you-are-really-hot-you-are-probably-better-off-not-wasting-your-2ddf370a6e9a (existence seen in search results; content not read in full).
**Hinge figures reported by Quartz (2017)** (D). Company analyst's Gini for likes received; reported values differ between retellings (0.54 or 0.73 for men). URL: https://qz.com/1051462/these-statistics-show-why-its-so-hard-to-be-an-average-man-on-dating-apps/ (not audited, not reproducible).
Relevance: these two are where the online "80/20" numbers come from. They describe attention inequality on apps, not partnering, and cannot be checked.

# Extension: beyond status (section 11 of the review)

Added 2026-10-28. Same format and grades as above. Every DOI was checked against Crossref on 2026-10-28; abstracts were read through OpenAlex, PubMed or Europe PMC. *(full text)* here means the full paper or an author version was read.

## Short-term vs long-term mating

**buss1993** (A). Theory paper with tests of 22 predictions from Sexual Strategies Theory, mostly US student questionnaires.
Men and women are proposed to have separate short-term and long-term strategies; preferences shift with mating context (for example, commitment and resources matter more for long-term mates) *(abstract)*.
The source of the "short-term vs long-term" frame. Mostly stated preferences from students.

**buss2019** (A). Annual Review of Psychology review.
Sex differences and similarities in mate preferences depend on short-term vs long-term context, mate value, sex ratio and economic inequality; reviews links from preferences to actual mating decisions *(abstract)*.
Current summary by the main proponents; useful as a map, not as independent evidence.

**kenrick1990** (B). 93 US undergraduates rated their minimum acceptable level on 24 traits at four levels of commitment (from a single date to marriage).
Women were more selective overall, especially on status-linked traits; men had much lower minimums for a sexual partner but were nearly as selective as women for a long-term partner *(abstract)*.
Classic evidence that the sex difference in choosiness is mostly a short-term phenomenon. Small student sample.

**likenrick2006** (B). Five studies with US students: mate-budget tasks, a mate-screening task and reasons for casual sex.
For long-term mates women treat status as a "necessity"; for short-term mates both sexes prioritise physical attractiveness first; both prefer well-rounded partners when budgets allow *(abstract)*.
Status preference is context-dependent: it is a long-term preference, not a general one. Stated trade-off tasks only.

**asendorpf2011** (B). Speed dating with one-year follow-up, German community sample, N = 382, ages 18 to 54.
Both sexes chose mainly on physical attractiveness; women also on men's education, income and some personality traits; 6% of matches led to sex and 4% to a relationship *(abstract)*.
Revealed choices in a non-student sample; shows status weights at the meeting stage and how rarely meetings turn into relationships.

**braun2006** (B). 239 US students (86% white) rated targets for a date, a one-time sexual encounter and a serious relationship.
Physical traits (women's waist-to-hip, men's waist-to-shoulder ratio) were valued most for one-time encounters; personality mattered more for serious relationships *(abstract)*.
Context effect again; also a body-build source.

**gildersleeve2014** (B). Meta-analysis of 134 effects from 50 studies (38 published, 12 unpublished) on ovulatory-cycle shifts.
Found cycle shifts toward cues of "genetic quality" when women rated short-term attractiveness, none for long-term attractiveness; bias tests did not explain the effect *(abstract)*.
Main evidence for the ovulatory-shift hypothesis; contradicted by wood2014 and later large preregistered studies.

**wood2014** (B). Meta-analysis of 58 reports (45 published, 13 unpublished).
Largely non-supportive: the few shifts looked like artefacts, declined over time, appeared only with loose fertile-window definitions and only in published work *(abstract)*.
The direct counter-meta-analysis; same literature, opposite conclusion.

**jones2018** (A). Longitudinal study, N = 584 women, salivary hormones, repeated face ratings.
No compelling link between hormonal changes and preference for facial masculinity; masculine faces were preferred more for short-term than long-term relationships *(abstract)*.
Largest test to date; the short-term vs long-term context effect survived, the cycle effect did not.

**stern2020** (A). Preregistered within-person study, N = 157 women, two cycles, LH tests and salivary hormones, video ratings of 70 men.
No cycle shift in preferences for competitive or courtship behaviour, for sexual or long-term attractiveness *(abstract)*.
Second large preregistered null; together with jones2018, moves the verdict toward "not robust".

## Height

**stulp2013paid** (B). Dutch student sample, N = 650, plus a published data set (N = 52,677) on height satisfaction.
Women were more selective and more consistent about partner height than men; women were most satisfied with a partner about 21 cm taller, men with a partner about 8 cm shorter *(secondary: repository abstract)*.
The "women want taller men more than men want shorter women" asymmetry. Student sample.

**stulp2013plos** (A). UK Millennium Cohort Study, 12,502 couples (parents), compared with simulated random pairing.
Height assortment r = .18; the man was not taller in 7.5% of couples vs 10.2% expected by chance; couples with the man more than 25 cm taller were also rarer than chance (13.9% vs 15.7%); preferences translate into pairing "only modestly" *(abstract and full text)*.
Key revealed evidence: the male-taller norm is real but is broken in about 1 in 13 couples, not far below chance.

**stulp2016** (A). Review of human height from an evolutionary perspective in Biological Reviews.
Selection acts on height through life history and possibly mate choice, but methodological problems limit firm conclusions *(abstract)*.
Background caution: even for an easily measured trait, links to mating success are uncertain.

**hancock2007** (C, conference paper). Online daters' profile claims compared with measured height, weight and age.
Most daters shaded the truth a little; men tended to overstate height *(secondary: search summary of the paper; percentages not checked)*.
Self-reported height on apps is inflated, so app "height effects" carry measurement error.

**xia2014** (C, arXiv preprint). Messaging data from a large Chinese dating site.
Men messaged younger women and women weighted income and education more; some apparent attribute preferences, including height, were close to what random selection would produce; stated and revealed preferences diverged *(abstract; height detail secondary)*.
A non-Western comparison and a warning to compare app behaviour with a random-selection baseline.

## Body build

**sell2017** (B). Three photo sets of men's bodies; separate raters judged strength or attractiveness.
Perceived upper-body strength explained over 70% of the variance in men's bodily attractiveness ratings; with height and leanness about 80%; no sign of a peak, the strongest bodies were rated most attractive *(abstract)*.
Strongest evidence that "frame" matters in ratings of bodies (photos, mostly students; not real partner choice).

**frederick2007** (B). Six studies, mostly US students.
Women rated muscular men as sexier but less committed; moderate muscularity was rated most attractive; women's recent short-term partners were more muscular than other partners (d = .36 and .47); muscular men reported more partners (partial r = .20 to .28) *(abstract)*.
Context effect for build (short-term) and a disagreement with sell2017 on whether "more" is always better.

**sorokowski2008** (B). 218 Polish volunteers rated silhouettes with altered leg length.
Legs about 5% longer than average were rated most attractive for both sexes; shorter and much longer legs were rated lower *(secondary: abstract copies and press)*.
The closest proxy for "wingspan"-type body proportions; no study on arm span was found.

## Penis size

**mautz2013** (B). Life-size projected computer-generated male figures (343 variants of height, shoulder-to-hip ratio and flaccid penis size) rated by Australian women (about 105, each saw 53 figures).
Larger penis size raised attractiveness with diminishing returns; its effect was about as large as height's; it mattered more in taller men and men with a more masculine body shape; body shape was the strongest predictor *(abstract; sample size and "body shape strongest" secondary: press)*.
The main experimental source. One lab, one country, flaccid size on figures, no real partners.

**prause2015** (B). 75 US women chose among 33 3D-printed erect models.
Preferred length 16.3 cm for a one-time partner vs 16.0 cm for a long-term partner (circumference 12.7 vs 12.2 cm); described as "only slightly larger than average" *(abstract)*.
The short-term vs long-term difference is tiny. Comparing these numbers with measured averages (veale2015, mean erect length 13.1 cm) is not straightforward: haptic models and clinical measurements differ.

**veale2015** (A). Systematic review of clinician-measured sizes, up to 15,521 men.
Mean erect length 13.12 cm (SD 1.66, n = 692), erect circumference 11.66 cm (SD 1.10, n = 381); length correlated with height at r = .2 to .6 *(abstract)*.
Reference values; shows most men are close to average and that size partly tracks height.

**lever2006** (B). Internet survey of 52,031 heterosexual US men and women (self-selected).
85% of women were satisfied with their partner's size; only 55% of men were satisfied with their own and 45% wanted to be larger *(abstract)*.
Men's concern about size is much greater than women's dissatisfaction: evidence that men overestimate its importance.

## Intelligence

**gignac2018** (B). Australian sample, N = 383, psychometric scale for sapiosexuality plus percentile ratings of partner IQ.
A minority report sexual attraction to intelligence; desirability of partner IQ peaked around the 90th percentile (about IQ 120) and fell slightly at the 99th; own measured IQ did not predict sapiosexuality *(secondary: press summaries; abstract not available)*.
Origin of the "ideal IQ about 120" claim. Stated ratings, one lab.

**gignac2019** (B). Replication with 214 young adults; IQ, kindness, easygoingness and physical attractiveness rated on percentile scales.
All four peaked at the 90th percentile; IQ and easygoingness dropped significantly from the 90th to the 99th, kindness and looks did not; participants' own intelligence was unrelated to their ratings *(abstract)*.
Supports a "threshold" for intelligence in stated preferences. Same lab, small samples.

**horwitz2023** (A). Systematic reviews and meta-analyses of 480 partner correlations from 199 studies, plus up to 79,074 UK Biobank couples.
Partner correlations: educational attainment r = .55, IQ r = .44 (95% CI .23 to .61, 17 estimates), height r = .24; in UK Biobank, education .48, a brief fluid IQ test .23, height .27 *(full text)*.
Strong evidence that partners are matched on education and intelligence: like with like, as for status.

**taylor2005** (B). Scottish Mental Survey 1932 linked to the Midspan studies, N = 883, born 1921.
Each standard deviation of childhood IQ lowered women's odds of ever marrying by mid-life (OR 0.42, 95% CI 0.27 to 0.64); in men the association was positive but not significant (OR 1.35, p = .07) *(secondary: abstract summary)*.
Old cohort, one country; shows that "intelligence predicts partnering" is not simple and may have differed by sex in the past.

## Looks, age targeting and online-dating data

**lee2008** (B). HOTorNOT.com data on ratings and dating requests.
Less attractive people chose less attractive dates but did not inflate how attractive those dates were; men's choices depended less on their own attractiveness than women's *(abstract)*.
How "low attractiveness" plays out in behaviour: people adjust whom they approach, men less than women.

**skopek2011age** (B). German online-dating behaviour plus a survey of stated preferences.
Men increasingly prefer younger women as they age; women's age preferences become more varied; age preferences are tangled with preferences for looks and education *(abstract)*.
Stated vs messaged age targeting; adds to kenrick1992 and antfolk2017.

**bruch2019** (A). Network analysis of messages among several million users of a large US dating site.
Markets split into submarkets by age and ethnicity; sex ratios differ between submarkets (younger ones have more men); racial stratification emerges from the messaging choices of both sexes *(abstract)*.
Shows that attention is organised by age and race, not by one global ranking only.

**hitsch2010qme, extra details** (A, already in the main list). Read in the January 2010 author version.
Height: women preferred men taller than themselves (marginal effects .050 for 2 to 5 inches taller, .071 for more than 5 inches) and men preferred shorter women; height preferences were almost entirely relative to one's own height. Looks: a man was 10.7% more likely to contact a woman in the 81st to 90th looks percentile than one in the lowest decile, a woman 7.9% for men; the sex difference was modest. Age: men disliked older women (-.054 for 5 to 9 years older, -.121 for 10 or more). Race: 30 of 31 race coefficients negative; a white man was 10% less likely to contact a black woman than a white woman, a white woman 12% less likely to contact an Asian man than a white man; women's same-race preferences were stronger *(full text, author version)*.
The often-quoted "6'3" men get 65% more messages than 5'7" men" figure comes from a 2006 draft; the 2010 papers say non-robust earlier results were dropped *(secondary for the draft figure)*.

## Race and ethnicity: documented bias

**lin2013** (A). Messages and replies among heterosexual users of a large US dating site.
Racial homophily dominated searching; in replies a racial hierarchy emerged: women replied to men of similar or more "dominant" racial status, non-black men replied to all but black women; education did not offset racial preferences *(abstract)*.
Core peer-reviewed documentation of racial bias in online dating.

**robnett2011** (B). 6,070 US internet dating profiles with stated racial preferences.
Whites were least open to dating across race; Asian and Latino patterns resembled whites'; exclusion was gendered: Asian men and black women were the most excluded *(abstract)*.
Stated exclusions; agrees with lin2013's revealed pattern.

**curington2021** (C, academic book). Book-length study of race and online dating in the US by sociologists (two are lin2013's authors).
Documents what the authors call digital-sexual racism: racialised and gendered hierarchies of attention, with black women and Asian men most disadvantaged *(secondary: publisher description; book not read)*.
Most complete synthesis; cited for framing, numbers taken from the articles instead.

**qian2011** (A). US Census 1980 and ACS 2008, newlyweds, log-linear models.
Black-white intermarriage tripled 1980 to 2008; intermarriage with whites stalled among Asians and American Indians; racial exogamy grew only after adjusting for changing opportunities *(abstract)*.
Actual marriages: homogamy by race is still strong but falling for some groups.

**rudder2014race** (D). OkCupid company blog, 2014.
Reported 2009 to 2014 attraction data: non-black men rated black women lower; women of all races preferred same-race men and rated Asian and black men lower; "if anything, racial bias has intensified a bit" *(secondary, archived copy; charts not read)*.
Public source of the viral race charts; agrees in direction with the peer-reviewed studies.
